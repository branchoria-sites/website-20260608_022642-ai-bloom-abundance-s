## Introduction

If advanced AI is going to help drive a wider human flourishing rather than simply enrich a handful of companies, access to computing [power]({{ 'power/' | relative_url }}) may matter almost as much as access to the models themselves. Modern AI depends on large clusters of specialised chips, data centres, storage systems and software infrastructure. Universities, public researchers, startups and civil society groups often cannot afford these resources at commercial scale. As a result, many of the most important AI experiments, models and scientific projects increasingly depend on a small number of private cloud providers.

<img src="{{ "/assets/images/AI_Bloom_Abun_98d3a6_governance_po_43cd85_public_comput_9e573e-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Public Compute illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/AI_Bloom_Abun_98d3a6_governance_po_43cd85_public_comput_9e573e-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/AI_Bloom_Abun_98d3a6_governance_po_43cd85_public_comput_9e573e-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
Public compute infrastructure is an attempt to change that balance. Instead of leaving advanced AI resources entirely in private hands, governments and public institutions fund supercomputers, research clouds and shared AI facilities that researchers can access through grants or allocation programmes. Supporters argue that this can widen participation in AI research, strengthen scientific independence and make it more likely that AI's benefits spread beyond a few dominant firms. Critics counter that public compute is expensive, difficult to manage and may never match the scale of the largest commercial platforms. The debate sits at the centre of a larger question: if [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) becomes a foundational resource for science, medicine and economic growth, who gets access to it?

## Why compute has become a gatekeeper

The idea that AI could contribute to abundance often focuses on software. Yet the most capable systems require enormous physical infrastructure. Training and deploying frontier models depends on advanced graphics processing units (GPUs), networking equipment, storage, electricity and engineering expertise.

This creates a practical access problem. A talented researcher may have a promising idea for drug [discovery]({{ 'discovery/' | relative_url }}), climate modelling or educational AI but still lack the resources needed to train or test sophisticated systems. In many fields, the bottleneck is no longer merely knowledge or talent. It is compute.

The argument is not that governments should replace private cloud providers. Rather, public compute is often presented as a counterweight: a way to prevent AI capability from becoming available only to organisations with vast capital resources. The Ada Lovelace Institute argues that expanding public compute can help exert greater public influence over the direction of AI development and increase the chances that AI serves broader social goals rather than purely commercial ones. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/the-role-of-public-compute/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adalovelaceinstitute.org">[Ada Lovelace Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adalovelaceinstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the role of public compute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ada Lovelace InstituteThe role of public compute24 Apr 2024 — Expanding public compute is one way of exerting greater public control over...</span></span></span>

Within the broader AI bloom vision, this matters because many of the hoped-for gains — accelerated science, medical breakthroughs, improved public services and educational tools — depend on who can experiment with advanced AI systems, not only who owns them.

## The US National AI Research Resource pilot

The most prominent American attempt to broaden AI access is the National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) Pilot, launched in 2024 under the leadership of the US National Science Foundation.

The programme emerged from concern that many researchers and educators lacked access to the computing resources needed for modern AI work. Rather than building a single government-owned supercomputer, the pilot uses a federated model that combines resources from government agencies, universities and private-sector partners. Researchers can apply for access to compute, datasets, models and technical support. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://data.aclum.org/storage/2025/01/NAIRR_new_nsf_gov_news_democratizing-future-ai-rd-nsf-launch-national-ai.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: data.aclum.org">[Data for Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">data.aclum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NAIRR new nsf gov news democratizing future ai rd nsf launch national ai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Resource pilot will give researchers access to critical data and compute, catalyzing action to achieve America&#x27;s great...Read more...</span></span></span> <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/ai/nairr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsf.gov">[NSF - U.S. National Science Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Science FoundationNational Artificial Intelligence Research ResourceVisit nairrpilot.org to explore opportunities for researcher...</span></span></span>

A notable feature of NAIRR is its attempt to lower barriers for researchers outside elite technology firms. Participating organisations contribute cloud credits, specialised hardware, datasets and AI tools. The goal is to give researchers opportunities that would otherwise require substantial private funding. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/ai/nairr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsf.gov">[NSF - U.S. National Science Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Science FoundationNational Artificial Intelligence Research ResourceVisit nairrpilot.org to explore opportunities for researcher...</span></span></span>

Supporters see several potential benefits:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* More universities can participate in frontier AI research.
* Public-interest projects can compete for resources.
* Students gain experience with advanced systems.
* Smaller institutions can contribute to scientific advances.
* Research agendas become less dependent on corporate priorities.

</div>

The programme also reflects a broader recognition that AI infrastructure itself has become a strategic national asset. NSF describes the pilot as a way to expand access to the computational, data and training resources needed to drive innovation and educate the next generation of researchers. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/ai/nairr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsf.gov">[NSF - U.S. National Science Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Science FoundationNational Artificial Intelligence Research ResourceVisit nairrpilot.org to explore opportunities for researcher...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the NAIRR model reveals a recurring tension in public compute policy. Much of the capacity still comes from private companies. That can make access easier and faster, but it also means public programmes may remain partly dependent on the same firms whose dominance they are trying to balance.

## The UK's AI Research Resource and sovereign compute ambitions

The United Kingdom has pursued a more infrastructure-focused approach through its AI Research Resource (AIRR).

AIRR is designed to provide AI-specialised computing capacity to researchers, universities, public institutions and businesses. The programme emerged from concerns that the UK faced a shortage of publicly available AI compute and risked falling behind countries with larger infrastructure investments. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-research-resource" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ai research resource</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">17 Jul 2025 — The AI Research Resource (AIRR) is a suite of advanced supercomputers that provides AI-specialised compute capacity to rese...</span></span></span>

Two flagship systems have become central to this effort:

* **Isambard-AI**, based in Bristol.
* **Dawn**, based at the University of Cambridge.

Together they form the backbone of Britain's [public AI]({{ 'public-ai/' | relative_url }}) supercomputing capability. The UK government has repeatedly described these systems as part of a broader effort to expand national compute capacity and reduce dependence on external infrastructure providers. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-research-resource/airr-advanced-supercomputers-for-the-uk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">airr advanced supercomputers for the uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">7 Nov 2025 — The AIRR programme intends to address the significant shortage of publicly available computing resources in the UK. Isambard-AI illustrates how public compute differs from traditional academic supercomputers. The system was designed specifically for AI workloads, with thousands of NVIDIA Grace Hopper processors and interfaces intended to be familiar to AI researchers accustomed to cloud platforms. Rather than requiring users to navigate older high-performance computing workflows, the system aims to support notebooks, containers and modern machine-learning pipelines. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.11199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isambard-AI: a leadership class supercomputer optimised specifically for Artificial IntelligenceOctober 15, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 15, 2024</span></span></span>

Government documents describe AIRR as a way to provide AI-specialised compute capacity not only to academia but also to industry, including startups and smaller firms. Dedicated programmes have offered hundreds of thousands of GPU hours to researchers and companies working on AI-related projects. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.ukri.org/opportunity/airr-compute-opportunity-ai-for-science/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ukri.org">[UK Research and Innovation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ukri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">airr compute opportunity ai for science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Research and InnovationAIRR compute opportunity: AI for Science21 Nov 2025 — Apply for between 200,000 and 1,000,000 graphics processi...</span></span></span>

The public justification goes beyond economic competitiveness. Policymakers increasingly frame compute as a form of national infrastructure, similar to scientific laboratories, [energy]({{ 'energy/' | relative_url }}) systems or telecommunications networks. The UK's stated ambition to expand AIRR capacity significantly by 2030 reflects a belief that access to compute may become a long-term determinant of scientific and economic power. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.ukri.org/opportunity/airr-compute-opportunity-ai-for-science/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ukri.org">[UK Research and Innovation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ukri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">airr compute opportunity ai for science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Research and InnovationAIRR compute opportunity: AI for Science21 Nov 2025 — Apply for between 200,000 and 1,000,000 graphics processi...</span></span></span>

## What public compute could unlock

The strongest case for public compute is not that it creates better chatbots. It is that it may broaden participation in the kinds of AI work most closely linked to long-term human flourishing.

### Scientific acceleration

Many of the most promising AI applications involve scientific research rather than consumer products. Drug discovery, materials science, fusion research, climate modelling and biological simulation increasingly rely on large-scale computation.

Research examining millions of scientific publications has found that work combining AI and high-performance computing is disproportionately associated with novel concepts and highly cited discoveries. The same research warns that unequal access to computational resources risks concentrating scientific opportunity in a small number of institutions and countries. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.11199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isambard-AI: a leadership class supercomputer optimised specifically for Artificial IntelligenceOctober 15, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 15, 2024</span></span></span>

If AI genuinely becomes a tool for accelerating discovery, then [broad access]({{ 'broad-access/' | relative_url }}) to compute may influence who contributes to those discoveries and which problems receive attention.

### Public-interest research

Commercial incentives do not always align with public priorities.

Researchers interested in rare diseases, neglected tropical illnesses, accessibility tools, public-sector applications or climate resilience may not attract the same investment as projects with obvious commercial returns. Public compute can create space for work that markets underfund.

The UK government has highlighted uses ranging from healthcare applications to environmental modelling, while researchers using Isambard-AI have pointed to projects involving medical diagnostics, materials science and agriculture. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/jul/17/ai-supercomputer-isambard-bristol-launches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Equipped with 5,400 Nvidia superchips, this high-performance machine runs 100,000 times faster than an average laptop and ranks as the 11...</span></span></span>

### Training future researchers

Access matters not only for today's breakthroughs but for building future capability.

A world in which only a handful of corporations can afford advanced AI infrastructure may also be a world in which the next generation of researchers receives most of its training inside those organisations. Public compute programmes can function as educational infrastructure, giving students and academic researchers hands-on experience with advanced systems. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/ai/nairr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsf.gov">[NSF - U.S. National Science Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Science FoundationNational Artificial Intelligence Research ResourceVisit nairrpilot.org to explore opportunities for researcher...</span></span></span>

That may become increasingly important if AI plays a growing role in science, engineering, medicine and public administration.

## Public compute is not automatically democratic

The phrase "public compute" can create a misleading impression that access is universal. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/the-role-of-public-compute/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adalovelaceinstitute.org">[adalovelaceinstitute.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adalovelaceinstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the role of public compute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ada Lovelace InstituteThe role of public compute24 Apr 2024 — Expanding public compute is one way of exerting greater public control over...</span></span></span>

In practice, these systems remain scarce resources. Someone must decide who receives allocation time, which projects qualify and what objectives justify public investment. Those decisions can create new forms of gatekeeping even when infrastructure is publicly funded.

Several tensions recur across public compute programmes: <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/the-role-of-public-compute/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adalovelaceinstitute.org">[adalovelaceinstitute.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adalovelaceinstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the role of public compute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ada Lovelace InstituteThe role of public compute24 Apr 2024 — Expanding public compute is one way of exerting greater public control over...</span></span></span>

**Allocation politics.** High demand means some projects will be prioritised over others. Governments and institutions must decide whether to favour commercialisation, scientific research, national security, public services or educational uses.

**Expertise barriers.** Access to hardware does not automatically create access to capability. Many organisations lack the engineers, data resources and specialised knowledge needed to use advanced systems effectively.

**Geographic concentration.** National programmes can widen access within wealthy countries while still leaving researchers in poorer regions excluded.

**Infrastructure dependence.** Even publicly funded programmes often rely on hardware, software or cloud services supplied by a small number of global technology firms.

As a result, public compute can reduce concentration without eliminating it. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/the-role-of-public-compute/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adalovelaceinstitute.org">[adalovelaceinstitute.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adalovelaceinstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the role of public compute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ada Lovelace InstituteThe role of public compute24 Apr 2024 — Expanding public compute is one way of exerting greater public control over...</span></span></span>

## The limits of building a public alternative

The biggest challenge facing public compute initiatives is scale. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/the-role-of-public-compute/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adalovelaceinstitute.org">[adalovelaceinstitute.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adalovelaceinstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the role of public compute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ada Lovelace InstituteThe role of public compute24 Apr 2024 — Expanding public compute is one way of exerting greater public control over...</span></span></span>

Frontier AI development increasingly requires enormous investments in chips, electricity and facilities. The largest technology companies spend tens of billions of dollars on AI infrastructure. Even ambitious national programmes may struggle to match that pace.

This creates a dilemma. [Public systems]({{ 'public-systems/' | relative_url }}) need to be large enough to remain useful, yet governments face budget constraints and competing priorities. Maintaining cutting-edge hardware also requires continual upgrades because AI infrastructure becomes outdated quickly.

Energy consumption is another constraint. Large AI supercomputers consume substantial power and require sophisticated cooling systems. Isambard-AI, for example, was designed with efficiency measures and low-carbon electricity, but operating advanced AI infrastructure remains expensive. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.itpro.com/infrastructure/inside-isambard-ai-the-uks-most-powerful-supercomputer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: itpro.com">[IT Pro]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">itpro.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Named after engineer Isambard Kingdom Brunel, the system offers 21 exaflops of AI performance and is ranked 11th globally. There is also a risk that public programmes become symbolic rather than transformative: impressive enough for national announcements but too small to materially change who controls the most powerful AI systems.

## Public compute versus public clouds

An important distinction is emerging between public compute and ordinary commercial cloud access.

Many researchers already rent computing resources from cloud providers. In one sense, cloud computing has democratised access by allowing organisations to rent infrastructure rather than buying entire supercomputers. Researchers have long noted that cloud-based high-performance computing can make powerful systems available to users who could never afford them directly. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.11199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isambard-AI: a leadership class supercomputer optimised specifically for Artificial IntelligenceOctober 15, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 15, 2024</span></span></span>

Yet cloud access does not necessarily solve the governance problem.

When a small number of firms control the infrastructure layer, they can influence prices, access conditions, technical standards and research dependencies. Public compute programmes are partly motivated by the belief that societies need at least some infrastructure that is governed through public institutions rather than entirely through commercial incentives.

This is increasingly linked to ideas of technological sovereignty. OECD analyses of AI compute infrastructure note that governments are paying growing attention to the location, ownership and governance of compute resources because they affect economic development, regulatory control and long-term strategic capacity. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/10/measuring-domestic-public-cloud-compute-availability-for-artificial-intelligence_39fa6b0e/8602a322-en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[OECD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Measuring domestic public cloud compute availability for...October 27, 2025 — by V Lehdonvirta · 2025 · Cited by 2 — Although public...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2025</span></span></span>

The underlying question is not simply whether researchers can rent compute. It is whether critical AI infrastructure remains accountable to democratic institutions as AI becomes more economically and scientifically important.

## Why this matters for an AI-enabled future

The optimistic vision of AI abundance often assumes that intelligence becomes widely available. Public compute is one of the practical policies aimed at making that assumption more plausible.

If advanced AI contributes to scientific acceleration, medical progress, climate solutions or educational empowerment, societies will face continual decisions about who gets access to the tools that enable those advances. Public compute does not guarantee fair distribution, nor does it solve deeper questions about wealth, ownership or AI safety. But it addresses a narrower and increasingly important issue: whether participation in the AI age depends entirely on access to a few private platforms.

The strongest argument for public compute is therefore not that governments should build every important AI system. It is that a civilisation hoping to benefit broadly from increasingly powerful machine intelligence may need at least part of its cognitive infrastructure to function as a public resource. Without that, many of the gains promised by AI abundance could arrive through institutions that are highly capable but only weakly accountable to the societies that depend on them.
