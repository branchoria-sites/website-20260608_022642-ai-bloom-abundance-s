## Introduction

The **Artemis Accords** are a set of non‑binding international principles first agreed in 2020 to guide cooperation in civil space exploration, resource utilisation, and safety practices among nations participating in lunar and deep‑space missions. While rooted in the 1967 **Outer Space Treaty’s** basic legal framework, the Accords have rapidly become a reference point for how states envisage **commercial activities and resource [control]({{ 'control/' | relative_url }}) beyond Earth**, especially for activities tied to NASA’s Artemis programme. As private companies and national space agencies prepare to harvest water ice, metals and other resources from the Moon, Mars, and asteroids, the Accords shape expectations about who may operate where and on what terms — with implications for **[who benefits]({{ 'who-benefits/' | relative_url }})** from the dawn of a potentially trillion‑dollar commercial space economy. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artemis Accords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artemis Accords</span></span></span>

## How the Accords Frame Commercial Space Control

At their core, the Artemis Accords present a **pragmatic framework** that interprets and applies existing space law to modern commercial and cooperative ventures. They codify principles such as peaceful use, transparency, interoperability of systems, and registration of objects — norms aimed at reducing operational risk and conflict among signatories. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artemis Accords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artemis Accords</span></span></span>

Crucially for commercial actors, Section 10 of the Accords addresses **space resource activities**, affirming that:

* Extraction and utilisation of space resources “can benefit humankind” and should comply with the Outer Space Treaty (OST), and
* Extracting resources “does not inherently constitute national appropriation” under the OST’s prohibition on claiming sovereignty over celestial bodies. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artemis Accords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artemis Accords - Wikisource, the free online libraryOctober 13, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 13, 2020</span></span></span>

By explicitly interpreting the treaty this way, the Accords **clear legal ambiguity** surrounding whether mining and resource use are permissible — a key uncertainty for investors and private firms. Without such affirmation, many operators and venture capital backers would lack confidence to commit capital to lunar and asteroid extraction projects. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cba.org/sections/air-and-space-law/resources/equity-in-the-space-frontier-the-laws-of-commercial-space-mining-and-solutions-for-common-benefit/?lang=en-ca" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cba.org">[Canadian Bar Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cba.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian Bar AssociationEquity in the Space Frontier: The laws of commercial space mining, and solutions for common benefit and sustainab...</span></span></span>

## Norms for Resource Extraction and Operational Control

The Accords’ resource‑related language acts less like a rigid legal code and more like a set of **norms and expectations** that participating states agree to build into their domestic laws and contracts with industry. They call on signatories to:

* Inform the **United Nations Secretary‑General** and the international scientific community about resource extraction activities in line with OST transparency requirements, and
* Use experience under the Accords to help build **multilateral rules** through bodies like the UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS). <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artemis Accords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artemis Accords - Wikisource, the free online libraryOctober 13, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 13, 2020</span></span></span>

This operational emphasis has two practical effects for commercial space control:

1. **Domestic implementation:** States become responsible for ensuring that companies operating under their flags comply with Artemis principles — making national legislation a key locus of control. Several signatory nations have already passed laws recognising private rights to extract and use space resources, explicit legal underpinnings that commercial actors can rely on. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cba.org/sections/air-and-space-law/resources/equity-in-the-space-frontier-the-laws-of-commercial-space-mining-and-solutions-for-common-benefit/?lang=en-ca" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cba.org">[Canadian Bar Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cba.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian Bar AssociationEquity in the Space Frontier: The laws of commercial space mining, and solutions for common benefit and sustainab...</span></span></span>
2. **Deconfliction and safety zones:** The Accords introduce concepts like “deconfliction” and measures to avoid harmful interference between activities — a practical mechanism to prevent collisions and disputes over where mining and settlement take place. While not legally binding, these norms set expectations for responsible behaviour. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-journal-of-international-law/article/signatories-of-the-usled-artemis-accords-meet-in-person-for-the-first-time/9637F1D9B1F81BCF5AA4AC659DA316AE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentSignatories of the U.S.-Led Artemis Accords Meet in Person for the First Time &#124; American Journal o...</span></span></span> University Press & Assessment

Together, these elements nudge governance away from vague treaty language toward **predictable commercial conditions** that early investors and operators often demand.

## Critiques: Power Concentration and Inclusivity

Despite their practical utility for industry, the Artemis Accords have attracted critique from some quarters concerned about **[power]({{ 'power/' | relative_url }}) concentration** and inclusivity in future space governance.

**1. Fragmentation of international law:** Legal scholars and diplomats note that the Accords operate outside the traditional multilateral framework of the United Nations. Unlike universally negotiated treaties, these bilateral and multilateral agreements led by the United States risk **fragmenting space governance**, with major space powers such as China and Russia remaining outside the club. This creates competing normative orders rather than a single, inclusive global regime. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/artemis-accords-and-next-generation-outer-space-governance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cfr.org">[Council on Foreign Relations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cfr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

**2. US‑centric and commercial tilt:** Critics argue the Accords reflect **American interpretations** of space law that are particularly favourable to commercial extraction. By reaffirming resource utilisation without broad international consensus, they may privilege firms and states with early access to technology and capital — reinforcing first‑mover advantages. Some states and commentators characterise this as prioritising US and Western commercial interests over a genuinely global common‑heritage approach. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artemis Accords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artemis Accords</span></span></span>

**3. Limited binding force and enforcement:** Because the Accords are voluntary and non‑binding, enforcement mechanisms are weak. Compliance depends on states’ willingness to implement norms domestically and through NASA contracts, which may leave gaps when private actors operate beyond direct state supervision or when signatory commitment wanes over time. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/artemis-accords-and-next-generation-outer-space-governance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cfr.org">[Council on Foreign Relations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cfr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

## Implications for Governance and Who Benefits

The Artemis Accords’ influence on commercial space control lies in their **norm‑setting role** at a pivotal moment in the industry’s growth. By clarifying that resource extraction is acceptable under treaty law, by anchoring expectations about transparency and safety, and by incentivising states to legislate accordingly, the Accords reduce early‑stage uncertainty that could otherwise chill investment and slow technological development. For many companies, this normative certainty is critical for planning and financing operations.

However, this comes with trade‑offs in terms of **global equity and long‑term governance coherence**. States outside the Accords may chart different regulatory paths, potentially leading to **competitive regimes** rather than a single shared framework. The resulting landscape could see powerful states and their corporate partners capturing key lunar and asteroid resources early — shaping settlement patterns, technology standards and economic returns in ways that are difficult to alter later. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/artemis-accords-and-next-generation-outer-space-governance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cfr.org">[Council on Foreign Relations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cfr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

Within the larger context of space settlement and human flourishing, these governance choices matter not merely for commercial actors but for the **distribution of opportunity and benefit**. If commercial space control becomes tightly aligned with a coalition of advanced economies and their firms, the broader promise of space as a frontier of shared human achievement may remain unrealised. The Accords do not settle this question; they make **specific governance bets** about the role of states and markets shaping the early stages of humanity’s expansion beyond Earth. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artemis Accords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artemis Accords</span></span></span>
