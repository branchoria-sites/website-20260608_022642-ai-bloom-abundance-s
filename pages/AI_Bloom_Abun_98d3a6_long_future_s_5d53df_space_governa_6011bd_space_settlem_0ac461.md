## Introduction

Human settlement beyond Earth — whether on the Moon, Mars or in orbital habitats — opens up one of the most profound opportunities for human civilisation’s long-term flourishing. Yet it also risks repeating, in a new domain, old patterns of inequality and exclusion if access to that future is confined to a narrow slice of humanity. The dream of space settlement carries with it questions not just about engineering life‑support systems or mastering interplanetary travel, but about who will actually get to live, work and shape those settlements — and on what terms. Because early space habitats will likely be extremely costly and technically demanding, initial participation could be shaped by wealth, nationality, employment and specialised skills in ways that reinforce terrestrial inequalities. Understanding these barriers — and how they might be addressed — is essential to any discussion about space settlement governance and whether an AI‑enabled era of abundance can be broadly shared rather than captured by a small elite.

## Cost and Skill Barriers for Settlers

One of the most immediate challenges to equitable access is economic cost. Settling space will require enormous resources to get people and supplies [off Earth]({{ 'civilisation-backup/' | relative_url }}), maintain [life support]({{ 'life-support/' | relative_url }}), and build infrastructure. Recent economic modelling suggests that for space settlement to attract a population on the order of a million people, the cost of living in space would need to fall by roughly **two orders of magnitude** from current expectations — something that is far from assured even with technological progress. Under plausible assumptions, early settlers are likely to *come from the upper tail of Earth’s income distribution*, because only those with high incomes and valuable skills would have the means and net benefit to take on the immense cost of relocating into space.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://spj.science.org/doi/full/10.34133/space.0479" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spj.science.org">[Science Advances]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spj.science.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science AdvancesAn Economic Framework for Space Immigration &#124; Space: Science &amp; TechnologyMarch 24, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 24, 2026</span></span></span>

Even if launch costs decline through advancements in reusable rockets, automation or AI‑driven productivity, the *net utility* of leaving Earth for space remains closely tied to income and skill. People with lower incomes could only participate if costs fall dramatically or if settlement becomes subsidised in ways that lower barriers to entry. Without such changes, selection effects mean that early space populations — and their opportunities — will be economically skewed, limiting access for much of humanity at first.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://spj.science.org/doi/full/10.34133/space.0479" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spj.science.org">[Science Advances]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spj.science.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science AdvancesAn Economic Framework for Space Immigration &#124; Space: Science &amp; TechnologyMarch 24, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 24, 2026</span></span></span>

Alongside cost, technical skill requirements also matter. Surviving in space will demand expertise in engineering, life support, medicine and habitat maintenance. Settlers are therefore likely to be drawn not just from the wealthy, but from those with specialised training — a pattern that could favour people from countries with advanced [education]({{ 'education/' | relative_url }}) systems and well‑funded space industries, further concentrating early access.

## Earth‑Based Power Imbalances and Structural Inequality

Barriers to equitable participation are not only economic but also political and structural. The current architecture of space governance and infrastructure reflects existing [power]({{ 'power/' | relative_url }}) imbalances among nation states and corporations. Access to launch facilities, funding, technology and institutional influence is heavily skewed toward established spacefaring nations and private firms with deep capital reserves. This uneven starting position shapes who can realistically participate in settlement initiatives.

Scholars working on space governance emphasise that Earth‑based politics, colonial legacies, and unequal distribution of scientific capacity already influence how space is accessed and [who benefits]({{ 'who-benefits/' | relative_url }}). Treaties like the Outer Space Treaty posit that space should be the “province of all mankind,” but they do not specify how benefits, access or participation should be allocated in practice. As a result, the governance of space is unfolding in a context where powerful states and firms have disproportionate influence over norms, regulatory frameworks and technological pathways.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://thecommonsjournal.org/articles/10.5334/ijc.1284" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecommonsjournal.org">[International Journal of the Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecommonsjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Journal of the CommonsAccessibility to Space Infrastructures and Outer Space: Anthropological Insights from Europe’s Spacep...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 26, 2023</span></span></span>

Voices from the Global South argue that current space governance structures risk extending terrestrial inequalities into space, with less developed countries having limited capacity to shape rules or secure meaningful participation. Making space a *global commons* in practice — not just in principle — will require deliberate governance to ensure that benefits and access are not controlled by a small set of wealthy actors.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/space-technologies/articles/10.3389/frspt.2025.1594352/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; Rebalancing space governance: a global south perspective on outer space as a global commonsJuly 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2025</span></span></span>

Furthermore, some critics note that a framework driven solely by private investment and national prestige could exacerbate inequities. In a scenario where profit motives or narrow political interests dominate, settlers and workers might find themselves in exploitative situations akin to historical patterns of enclosure and exclusion, with limited agency and high dependence on sponsoring organisations.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016328722001732" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Space not for everyone: The problem of social exclusion in the concept of space settlement - ScienceDirectJanuary 1, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2023</span></span></span>

## Policies for Equitable Participation

Addressing these challenges requires policies and governance mechanisms designed with equity at their core, rather than treating fairness as a by‑product of technical progress. A range of ideas have been proposed to make early settlement more inclusive:

* **Subsidised Access and Diversity Quotas:** If governments or international bodies subsidise seats for settlers, they could include provisions to ensure representation from less wealthy countries, underrepresented groups and people without high incomes. Subsidy schemes could mirror models in international development or scientific collaborations that broaden participation.
* **Global Governance Frameworks:** Strengthening international governance — for example through updated treaties or norms that explicitly embed principles of equity — could help ensure that settlement opportunities are not monopolised by a few states or corporations. Norms inspired by *global commons* frameworks emphasise collective benefit and participatory decision‑making, potentially redistributing influence more evenly.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://thecommonsjournal.org/articles/10.5334/ijc.1284" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecommonsjournal.org">[International Journal of the Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecommonsjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Journal of the CommonsAccessibility to Space Infrastructures and Outer Space: Anthropological Insights from Europe’s Spacep...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 26, 2023</span></span></span>
* **Educational and Capacity‑Building Initiatives:** Investing in education, technology transfer and capacity building in countries with limited space infrastructure could diversify the pool of people capable of participating in settlement projects. By lowering skill barriers, such initiatives increase the likelihood that space remains a site of shared endeavour rather than a gated frontier.
* **Ethical Governance Mechanisms:** Embedding ethical oversight and stakeholder representation into space agencies and commercial ventures can foreground fairness, transparency, and shared benefit. Such mechanisms might draw on analogous fields like environmental governance, where inclusive processes aim to balance competing interests while protecting broader societal interests.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/space-technologies/articles/10.3389/frspt.2025.1594352/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; Rebalancing space governance: a global south perspective on outer space as a global commonsJuly 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2025</span></span></span>

It is also worth considering institutional models that distribute decision‑making power more broadly from the outset. In Earth history, inclusive governance of shared resources often depends on multi‑stakeholder institutions that ensure a variety of voices are heard and interests considered. Translating this to the space context could help prevent the concentration of benefits among early settlers and their sponsors.

## Conclusion

The prospects for human settlement beyond Earth carry immense promise, but without careful attention to equity and access, they risk reinforcing and extending terrestrial inequalities into space. High costs, skill barriers, and entrenched power imbalances mean that early settlers are likely to come from wealthy, well‑resourced backgrounds and countries unless governance efforts deliberately broaden participation. Embedding equity‑focused policies — through subsidies, international frameworks, inclusive governance structures and capacity building — will be crucial to ensuring that space settlement contributes to a genuinely shared human future, aligning up‑front access with the broader ideals of human flourishing and AI‑enabled long‑term expansion.
