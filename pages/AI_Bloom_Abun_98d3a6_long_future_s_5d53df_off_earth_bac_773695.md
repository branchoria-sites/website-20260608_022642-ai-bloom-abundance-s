## Introduction

The notion that *off‑Earth settlements* could act as a **backup for civilisation** is one of the most striking and long‑running arguments for expanding humanity beyond this planet. At its core, this idea holds that if human life, culture, technology and knowledge were present in more than one celestial environment—be that the Moon, Mars, free‑floating orbital habitats, or broader networks of communities in space—then a single global catastrophe on Earth would not automatically wipe out civilisation’s achievements and potential. This concept resonates with deep concerns about existential and civilisational risks, from natural disasters to anthropogenic threats, and ties into long‑run debates about survival, [resilience]({{ 'resilience/' | relative_url }}), and how far human ambition should extend in space. But as we examine this *backup* rationale more closely, its appeal and feasibility depend crucially on how independent such settlements could ever be, what forms they might take, and what major limits and debates shape the argument. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_settlement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Space settlement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Space settlement</span></span></span>

## What the Backup Argument Claims

Proponents of off‑Earth settlement often frame it as a safeguard against civilisation‑ending events on Earth. **Existential risks**—from massive asteroid impacts and supervolcanic eruptions to engineered pandemics or catastrophic nuclear war—could, in theory, devastate terrestrial human life. A widely cited motivation for some form of settlement is that if parts of our civilisation were off the planet, **the whole would not disappear in a single catastrophe**. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2624-6120/3/4/49" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MDPIA Lunar Backup Record of Humanity | MDPINovember 7, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 7, 2022</span></span></span>

This *redundancy* idea extends beyond resilient human populations to preserving knowledge, culture and technology. For example, schemes have been proposed to use lunar bases to store vast data archives of human achievements and historical records, enabling recovery after a global collapse. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2624-6120/3/4/49" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MDPIA Lunar Backup Record of Humanity | MDPINovember 7, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 7, 2022</span></span></span>

Advocates also argue that establishing space settlements encourages development of **ecosystem management, resource recycling and autonomous life‑support technologies** that could benefit Earth and help civilisational resilience more generally. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.thespacereview.com/article/2915/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thespacereview.com">[The Space Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thespacereview.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Space ReviewThe Space Review: Settling space is the only sustainable reason for humans to be in spaceFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

## How Independent a Settlement Would Need to Be

The strength of the ‘backup’ rationale rises or falls on **whether an off‑Earth settlement could be genuinely autonomous**—that is, able to survive without regular supplies from Earth when Earth itself fails.

At minimum, a settlement claiming to *back up civilisation* would need to:

* **Produce its own essentials**: food, water, breathable air and [energy]({{ 'energy/' | relative_url }}).
* **Manufacture tools and infrastructure**: including spare parts, construction materials and advanced machinery.
* **Maintain stable populations**: with demographic viability over generations.
* **Retain cultural and informational continuity**, such as [education]({{ 'education/' | relative_url }}) systems, records and technological expertise.

Earth‑dependent [scientific]({{ 'discovery/' | relative_url }}) bases and small outposts fall short of these criteria. The distinction between a *base* and a *true settlement* rests on whether a community can argue it is on a path to independence from Earth’s support. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nss.org/nss-roadmap-to-space-settlement-3rd-edition-2018-part-6/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nss.org">[NSS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nss roadmap to space settlement 3rd edition 2018 part 6</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSSNSS Roadmap to Space Settlement (3rd Edition 2018): Part Six: Additional Expansion and Greater Sustainability of Human Civilization – NSS...</span></span></span>

### Dependence on Local Resources and Economy

A key factor in independence is *in situ resource utilisation* (ISRU): the ability to use materials found where the settlement is located. On Mars or the Moon, this might include extracting water from ice, generating oxygen from local minerals, and sourcing construction materials for habitat expansion. But even if such resources are tapped, turning raw materials into complex goods without Earth factories or advanced [supply chains]({{ 'risky-elements/' | relative_url }}) presents enormous challenges still far from resolved. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273117720302234" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirectLunar and off Earth resource drivers, estimations and the development conundrum - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2020</span></span></span>

Economists studying space settlement emphasise that without robust economic activity—whether mining, manufacturing or trade—even long‑lasting communities may struggle to sustain themselves in any meaningful sense. They argue that [voluntary]({{ 'lab-pressure/' | relative_url }}) migration and economic rationality are prerequisites for large‑scale independent settlement. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://spj.science.org/doi/full/10.34133/space.0479" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spj.science.org">[Science Advances]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spj.science.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science AdvancesAn Economic Framework for Space Immigration | Space: Science &amp; TechnologyMarch 24, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 24, 2026</span></span></span>

### Genetic and Sociological Viability

For a settlement to persist beyond simple survival, questions arise about the **minimum population size** and sociological structures needed to maintain genetic diversity, cultural continuity, and technological progress. Simulation studies suggest that even relatively small isolated groups require careful planning to avoid demographic risks and social stresses, and that self‑sufficiency remains far from assured. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05916" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv An Exploration of Mars Colonization with Agent-Based Modeling</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXivAn Exploration of Mars Colonization with Agent-Based ModelingAugust 11, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 11, 2023</span></span></span>

## Failure Modes, Timelines and Limits

The backup argument faces several serious **practical and philosophical challenges**:

**Technological Immediacy vs. Long Horizons:** Many technical obstacles—radiation protection, closed‑loop [life support]({{ 'life-support/' | relative_url }}), manufacturing at scale in hostile environments—are not solved and may take centuries to reach maturity. Plans that treat these challenges as near‑term risks can overstate their closeness. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273117720302234" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirectLunar and off Earth resource drivers, estimations and the development conundrum - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2020</span></span></span>

**Cost‑Benefit Trade‑offs:** Some analyses suggest that improving Earth’s resilience—through planetary defence systems, climate adaptation, improved global health and peace initiatives—offers more immediate and cost‑effective mitigation of existential risk than building off‑Earth colonies. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/mars-can-wait-questions-surround-settlements-on-other-worlds/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scientificamerican.com">[Scientific American]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scientificamerican.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientific AmericanMars Can Wait. **Dependency Persistence:** Critics argue that early settlements will be so dependent on Earth that they offer limited protection against civilization‑ending events affecting Earth’s economy or infrastructure. ## Assessment of the Backup Rationale

In practice, the idea of off‑Earth settlements as a civilisation backup sits between **long‑term aspiration and distant feasibility**. It represents a compelling vision for reducing the risk that a single planetary catastrophe could erase humanity’s cultural and scientific march. At the same time, the **independence threshold** that would make a settlement a true backup is far beyond current or near‑term capability, requiring technological, economic and demographic breakthroughs that may unfold over centuries—even if political will and resources were abundant.

For thinkers interested in the far future of civilisation and the role of advanced technologies, this backup argument serves as a **useful lens** on how space settlement could contribute to civilisational resilience—while also reminding us that protecting Earth and building distributed capacities here remains vital.
